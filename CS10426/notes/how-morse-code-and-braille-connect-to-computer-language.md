## How Morse code and Braille connect to computer language?

Morse code, Braille, and computers represent different forms of information
representation, using a limited set of signals. In Morse, you can get an
information like the letter 'A' and turn into a sequency of signals like `.-`;
in Braille, the letter is transformed into a specific spatial combination of
raised and unraised dots; the same concept applies to computers, except that
two states (0 and 1) are used to represent information. Therefore, when you see
`01000001,` it is not inherently “the letter A”, it is a representation that,
following a specific rule (ASCII), signifies 'A', just as `.-` signifies 'A' when
using Morse code. The fundamental idea behind all three is precisely to encode
information: **establishing a rule that allows us to transform something we wish
to communicate into a combination of signals, and then interpret that
combination to retrieve the original information.**

In the same way, simple electric circuits can also be understood through two
distinct states: on and off. Electricity is essentially the movement of
electric charge through a conducting material. In a simple circuit, this
movement can be controlled by a switch, which either allows the current to flow
or prevents it from flowing. In this sense, the circuit can represent a binary
state: on or off.

Just as Morse code uses dots and dashes and Braille uses raised and unraised
dots to distinguish between different pieces of information, an electric
circuit can use the presence or absence of current to represent information.
This connection is fundamental to understanding how computers use physical
electrical states to represent and process information.
